# Research Notes: The Development of Artificial Intelligence

Research date: 2026-10-04

## 1. Historical Development

- **1936 / 1950 - Turing.** Alan Turing introduced the theoretical computing machine (1936) and published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), posing the question "Can machines think?" and proposing the imitation game (Turing Test).
- **1956 - Dartmouth Conference.** The Dartmouth Summer Research Project, with John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky and Claude Shannon, is regarded as the birth of AI as a discipline. McCarthy proposed the name "artificial intelligence".
- **2012 - AlexNet.** Alex Krizhevsky (a doctoral student of Geoffrey Hinton) won the ImageNet competition by a large margin with a deep-learning model, igniting the deep learning boom in image recognition, speech recognition, NLP and game playing.
- **2017 - Transformer.** Google researchers published "Attention Is All You Need", introducing the Transformer and its attention mechanism, which became the basis of modern large language models.

## 2. Current Technology Trends (2025-2026)

- **Accelerating capability.** According to the Stanford AI Index 2026, industry produced over 90% of notable frontier models in 2025. Several models meet or exceed human baselines on PhD-level science questions, multimodal reasoning and competition mathematics. Performance on SWE-bench Verified rose from about 60% to near 100% in one year.
- **Agentic AI.** AI systems increasingly execute multi-step workflows autonomously; one source reports that about 33% of enterprise software includes agentic capabilities. Specialized multi-agent teams are replacing single "super-agents".
- **Multimodal models.** Models that process text, images, audio and video together are built into the core APIs of major cloud providers.
- **Test-time reasoning.** Extended reasoning at inference (for example DeepSeek-R1) improves accuracy on complex queries.
- **Voice and edge.** Real-time speech-to-speech models with sub-second latency are spreading, and agents are moving to local (edge) devices.
- **Geopolitics.** The U.S.-China gap in top model performance has nearly closed (the top U.S. model led by 2.7% as of March 2026).

## 3. Industrial and Social Impact

- **Adoption.** Generative AI reached 53% population-level adoption, faster than the personal computer or the internet (AI Index 2026).
- **Investment.** U.S. private AI investment reached $285.9 billion in 2025. The estimated U.S. consumer surplus from generative AI was $172 billion (up 54%).
- **Productivity.** Reported gains: 14-15% in customer support, 26% in software development, up to 50% in marketing.
- **Economic potential.** McKinsey estimates generative AI could add $2.6-4.4 trillion annually. Sales and marketing, software engineering, customer operations and product R&D account for about 75% of the gains. Generative AI could automate activities that take 60-70% of employees' time, with the greatest effect on higher-wage knowledge work.
- **Labor market.** Large-scale job losses are not yet visible in overall statistics, but one-third of surveyed organizations expect to reduce their workforce in the coming year, mostly in service operations, supply chain and software engineering.

## 4. Risks and Regulation

- **EU AI Act.** Most remaining obligations apply from 2 August 2026 (Annex III high-risk systems, Article 50 transparency rules). Article 6(1) obligations apply from 2 August 2027.
- **Korea AI Basic Act.** In force since 22 January 2026. It requires risk assessments and a Korea-based local representative for high-impact and generative AI. A one-year grace period focuses on guidance rather than penalties. Enhanced obligations apply above a compute threshold of 10^26.
- **Open issues (general knowledge).** Reliability and hallucination, bias, labor displacement, concentration of capital and compute, and the need for transparency.

## 5. Outlook

- Capability and adoption continue to grow; competition shifts toward agents, reasoning and efficient deployment.
- Regulation is moving from preparation to enforcement.
- Benefits depend on how productivity gains are distributed and how workers are reskilled.
